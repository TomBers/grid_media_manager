### 3. If you are right by luck, do you really know? · LinkedIn

2026-11-12T15:30:00Z · Buffer `6af310676202102f59bf0b65` · [Source](https://rationalgrid.ai/g/if-you-are-right-by-luck-do-you-really-know-c566bc)

Right by luck — is that knowledge? Consider Henry driving through a landscape of convincing fake barns. He looks, sees a genuine barn and forms the belief “there is a barn.” He has justification and his belief is true. Yet many philosophers hesitate to call this knowledge. Why?

The core worry is that knowledge demands more than a true belief backed by reasons; it requires that the belief be produced by a process that tracks the truth across nearby possible situations. Henry’s perceptual process is successful only because the world happened to place a real barn in his line of sight. If he’d looked slightly elsewhere, he would have formed the same confident belief about a façade. That sort of environmental luck dissolves the stable connection we expect between the world and our cognitive methods.

This reliability‑based diagnosis helps in other domains, too. In science, a model might hit a correct prediction only because two distinct errors cancel each other out. Practical success is real, but many argue it is epistemically hollow: the method does not reliably produce truth across counterfactuals and therefore does not amount to knowledge.

The important qualification is methodological: reliabilist solutions depend on how we draw the relevant counterfactuals. Make them too narrow and luck looks harmless; make them too broad and almost nothing qualifies as knowledge. Given that tension, should an account of knowledge prioritise counterfactual stability even at the cost of excluding many ordinary true beliefs?
